Owning and maintaining a heritage building can be quite tricky if you’re not in the know. One thing that homeowners struggle with is finding reliable tradesmen who have a good understanding of the correct materials. Alongside the use of synthetic insulation and plastic coatings, one of the biggest concerns is the use of cement mortars instead of lime mortars. 

Although there were instances of earlier use, modern cement didn’t come to prevalence in the UK until the post-WWII boom and was considered a wonder material at the time because of its strength and fast setting times, compared to traditional lime mortars. It’s only in the past 20-or-so years that building conservation professionals are understanding the impact that hard modern cement, and by extension other modern materials such as silicone renders, have on adjacent and underlying masonry. The problem with this is that now the majority of tradesmen have worked with cement, their fathers and their grandfathers used it, they have ’40 years experience’ using it and are unaware of the new research being done. Cement based products are cheap, easy to work with and easy to get hold of so it’s no wonder builders insist on using them. 

This is compounded by an education system that focusses on building new houses instead of correctly retrofitting existing vacant units; something which I believe is sorely needed in the UK. We have the oldest housing stock in Europe and it’s mostly Victorian and Edwardian terraces thanks to the UK being the heart of the industrial revolution.

This is by no means a post to tear down your average tradesperson – I can’t imagine anyone wants to put in a full day’s hard graft to then come home and read the latest research paper by Historic England or the SPAB. The intention of this post is to draw a homeowner’s attention to the where issues might occur if we don’t understand the technicalities of old buildings, so they can better advocate for their buildings when discussing their project.

Cement is a great material to use on modern buildings that do not need to breath, have movement joints built in and have cavities and DPCs to prevent moisture getting into the building. They then have extract fans internally to get moisture out. However, I’m such a massive advocate for lime that I’d suggest using it on new buildings as well – you won’t need those unsightly movement joints and it’s so much better for the environment as a hydraulic lime sets by taking carbon out of the air!

**So how do you know if your building is pointed or rendered with lime or cement?**

The best thing to do is to take a sample and get it tested professionally – there are a number of companies online and you’ll get a better understanding of what the mortar mix is made up of and can try to replicate it like-for-like. This will ensure the mortar or render is technically appropriate for your building and it will give you a better colour match. Alternatively, you can put some of your mortar in a small dish of white vinegar at home. If the mortar fizzes then this is the alkaline lime reacting with the acidic vinegar – cement wouldn’t have the same reaction.

**But what if my old building has already been repointed or rendered with modern cement?**

It can be repaired, and you will likely have some of the original mortar deeper within the mortar joint that can be removed and tested. Just ensure that any cement mortar is removed carefully to prevent damage to brickwork.

**But my building isn’t listed.**

A humble two-up-two-down Victorian workers terrace doesn’t know it’s listed any more than Windsor Castle does, it’s about the materials that it’s made of and how it performs technically. General rule of thumb for rendering, plastering and pointing: if you have solid walls use a hot-mix non-hydraulic lime or lime putty, if you have modern cavity walls use cement and gypsum. Natural Hydraulic Lime (NHL) has its place but should be used with caution as there has been research done by well-respected heritage stonemasons, such as Nigel Copsey that show that over time NHLs can set harder than cement. There are practical examples of this at places like Lincoln Castle where NHL mortars have caused soft stonework to delaminate. If you’re unsure, then steer clear.

**I have a cavity wall, do I still need to use lime?**

With cavity walls one might assume that the air gap will prevent moisture from crossing to the inner leaf of brickwork and effecting the internal environment, however, there are a few other things that need to be considered.

Firstly, when cavities were first introduced in the late 1800s, and most certainly by the 1930s cement was being used in mortar mixes alongside lime, albeit in small quantities, which makes getting mortar of this age lab-tested even more important.

Secondly, cavity ties - old ties in an early 1900s house will be made from iron or similar perishable metals; trapping moisture in the external leaf through poor mortar specification and increasing humidity within the cavity will have an impact on the wall ties and in the worst case could lead to structural failure. Furthermore, increasing humidity in this space can cause rot on structural joist bearings that sit on the inner leaf of masonry.

**Are traditional materials expensive?**

Yes and no – An NHL lime would cost about 4x more than a bag of cement, and lime putty can be expensive too, but quicklime for a hot lime mix is comparable to cement when considering it expands to twice it’s volume when it slakes (slaking is the process when water is added to quicklime and it gives off heat, hence “hot lime mix”). The main cost comes from the labour as there is a bit of ‘aftercare’ required when working with lime; and considering traditional skills are harder and harder to come by, this is understandable.

The other thing to consider is that using the proper materials, you’re unlikely to see the problem again in your lifetime as the materials should outlast you; if you use the wrong materials then it might only be a year until you start getting knock-on problems with damp and mould, which further wastes time and money.

**So what about the time it takes?**

Working with lime does take longer than using modern materials because of the aftercare that is required, which is a real sticking point for some customers as this means scaffolding is left up longer for external works, or there is more upheaval for internal works. Unfortunately, there isn’t a way around this and it’s why projects need to be carefully considered and phased to mitigate inconvenience to owners, especially if they’re living in the building during works.

I have seen instances with render where a small area of render has failed and run-of-the-mill builders have suggested the whole lot needs to come off to re render it in cement. If you get someone in who understands the use of a hot-mix lime it’s likely they will be able to patch the problem area. It would be quicker and cheaper than replacing the whole lot and I can guarantee a modern render would blow again within 20 years (and that’s being generous!)

If you want more information, there are a few books I always recommend. For the complete novice, I’d spend £30 on a copy of the SPAB Old House Handbook by Roger Hunt & Marianne Suhr (https://uk.bookshop.org/p/books/old-house-handbook-a-practical-guide-to-care-and-repair-2nd-edition-roger-hunt/7415760?aid=13434&ean=9780711281479&) which talks about this and other maintenance issues encountered with an old house in layman’s terms. I’d recommend this book to all old house owners and to the builders that are working on them; it’s a great reference book before approaching tradesmen or doing your own DIY.

For something a bit more in-depth, homeowners should get a copy of The Warm Dry Home by Pete Ward (https://shop.heritage-house.org/product/book-the-warm-dry-home/). I can’t recommend this book enough, it’s a real primer in ensuring that your old house works as it should throughout the year through all of our Great British seasons.

And if you’re wanting something that’s very technical backed up with a whole lot of research, then get Hot Mixed Lime and Traditional Mortars: A Practical Guide to Their Use in Conservation and Repair by Nigel Copsey (https://www.summerfieldbooks.com/product/hot-mixed-lime-and-traditional-mortars-a-practical-guide-to-their-use-in-conservation-and-repair/?srsltid=AfmBOopAVzmbZMg227ty-_fGwmfEK7_1-8AjKzjOfTfimtFU200bZ2HW)

Historic England have also posted a series of lectures on their website regarding damp in buildings, which may be of interest to people too: https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/technical-advice/buildings/damp-masonry-decay-and-sustainability-conference
